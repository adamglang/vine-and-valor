### Why freeze-thaw is the silent killer up here

If you've lived a few winters in Priest River, Sandpoint, or up at Priest Lake, you've watched concrete come apart. Surface scaling. Edges crumbling. Cracks that open in March and widen every year. None of that is the fault of "concrete" as a material it's the fault of concrete that was never poured for the climate it sits in.

North Idaho runs through dozens of freeze-thaw cycles every winter. Water soaks into the pore structure of the slab during the day. It freezes at night, expands nine percent, and pries the slab apart from the inside. Do that fifty or a hundred times a year and a slab with no air entrainment will start spalling in three seasons.

###### The concrete matters more than the contractor will admit

A good freeze-thaw mix has four things working together:

- **Air entrainment in the 5–7 percent range**, giving the water somewhere to go when it freezes.
- **A lower water-to-cement ratio** (around 0.40–0.45) so the slab is dense, not thirsty.
- **A higher strength class** 4,000 psi or better for driveways and exterior flatwork.
- **Supplementary cementitious materials** like fly ash or slag where the project supports it.

That's a very different mix from the 3,000 psi interior pad somebody cut-and-pasted onto your exterior spec.

###### Curing is free and nobody does it

A freshly poured slab wants to lose its water in the first week. If you let it, the surface ends up thirsty, weak, and the first layer to scale the following winter. Proper curing wet cure, cure blankets, or a curing compound keeps the hydration reaction running long enough to develop real surface strength.

We cure every exterior pour. It takes a few minutes and it doubles the useful life of the concrete.

###### Then you seal it

A penetrating sealer (silane or siloxane, not a topical) keeps liquid water from soaking in and freezing inside the slab. Reapply every two to five years depending on traffic and UV. Cheap insurance for an expensive pour.
